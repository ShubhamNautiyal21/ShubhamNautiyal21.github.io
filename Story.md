# The Grief Eaters

## I. Crowded

Mira hated people.

She said it the way she said most true things lately — flatly, like a fact she'd already grieved. She was standing at the mouth of the metro station at eight in the morning, watching the crowd break against the turnstiles and reassemble on the other side, a hundred strangers moving with the same tired urgency, the same practiced indifference to each other's elbows and bags and breath.

"So much of crowd," Ayesha said beside her, not really disagreeing, just noting it, the way you'd note weather.

Mira didn't answer. She watched a man shoulder past an old woman without looking at her, watched two schoolgirls laughing at something on a phone screen while the world rushed around them unnoticed, and felt something in her chest pull tight and small, like a fist closing.

It hadn't always been like this. She used to love mornings like this one — the noise of it, the aliveness of it. But that was a different version of the crowd, in a different version of her life, and she didn't like to think too long about the difference.

They pushed through the gates together. Ayesha talked about nothing in particular — a show she was watching, a landlord problem, the price of vegetables — filling the silence the way she'd been doing for months now, ever since she'd learned that silence, left alone with Mira for too long, tended to curdle into something neither of them wanted to sit with.

---

The office was on the eleventh floor of a glass building that trapped heat like a greenhouse. Mira reached her desk at 9:02, two minutes late, and was already tired.

Across the room, Priya was leaning over their boss's desk in a way that had nothing to do with the quarterly report open between them — a hand on his shoulder, a laugh half a second too long, a tilt of the head that put her hair exactly where the light would catch it. Mira watched without meaning to. She'd stopped being surprised by it months ago; what she hadn't stopped being was tired of it.

Ten minutes later their boss walked past Mira's desk and dropped two folders in front of her — the "Whitmore" account and a follow-up file that anyone with functioning eyes could see belonged to Priya's workload, not hers.

"Priya's swamped," he said, already walking away. "You've got bandwidth."

Mira didn't have bandwidth. She had exactly the amount of bandwidth required to survive her own life, and none left over for someone else's flirtation tax. She looked over and caught Priya smiling into her coffee cup from the corner of the break room, and something old and exhausted in Mira just — sighed.

"I hate my life," she told Ayesha at lunch, not dramatically, just as a fact laid on the table between them like the two lukewarm sandwiches neither of them was eating.

Ayesha reached over and squeezed her hand. "I know," she said. "I know. It's okay. It's going to be okay."

It was the kind of thing you said when there was nothing true left to say, and they both knew it, and neither of them minded, because sometimes a kind lie is the only thing holding a person together at 1 p.m. on a Tuesday.

## II. The Ride Home

The metro home was worse than the metro in, because the metro home had memories in it.

Mira found her usual seat — the one she'd started taking eight months ago, three cars back from where she used to sit — and let her head rest against the window as the train pulled out of the station and the city blurred into smears of light.

It had started here, hadn't it. Not the relationship — that had started somewhere softer, a mutual friend's birthday, a conversation about a band neither of them actually liked that much — but the *life* of it, the daily fabric of it, had been woven on this train. Two years of mornings where she used to fight him, laughing, for the window seat. Two years of his hand finding hers under a bag on his lap so no one would see, because he was shy like that, sweetly, stupidly shy for a man who otherwise walked through the world like he owned it.

She used to love the crowd back then. Used to love the crush of bodies at rush hour because it meant they had an excuse to stand closer, his chest against her back, his mouth close enough to her ear that she could feel him breathing before she heard what he said. She used to think: *this is what people mean, when they say they're happy.* All of it — the noise, the smell of a hundred strangers' coffee, the ordinary chaos of a city trying to get to work — all of it had been beautiful, because he was in it with her.

They used to come in together every morning. Used to sit in the same two seats, third row, aisle and window, like it was reserved for them by some silent agreement with the universe. He used to bring her a coffee from the stand outside her building even though he had to backtrack ten minutes to do it, and he never once mentioned the ten minutes, like it wasn't a sacrifice at all, like it was just what you did for someone you loved.

The train jolted through a tunnel and the lights flickered, and for a moment she saw her own reflection ghosted over the glass, and behind it, unbidden, the memory that undid all the others: the photo. Not even a scandalous one. Just two people, her boyfriend and a girl from his gym, standing too close at a party Mira hadn't been invited to, his hand resting on the small of her back in a way that hands don't rest on friends.

She'd found it by accident. That was the cruelty of it — she hadn't gone looking. She'd just been holding his phone to change the music, and there it was, sitting in a chat thread with three months of context she'd never suspected, and her whole life had cracked open like a dropped plate.

Ayesha had been there for the breakup — sat with her on the bathroom floor while Mira cried in a way she didn't know a body could cry, in heaves that felt like they might turn her inside out. What made it worse, what made it almost unbearable, was that Ayesha had also been *his* friend, first, before she'd been Mira's — the three of them had orbited each other for years — and so even Ayesha's comfort came tangled with a grief of its own, a loyalty split down the middle that she never once let show.

He used to play with Mira's hair. Late at night, half-asleep, his fingers would find their way into it without either of them deciding it, and he'd say, so quietly she sometimes thought she'd imagined it, *I love you, you know that.* She'd cut her hair the week after the breakup — not a trim, a full break, shoulder-length gone to a blunt bob that made her mother gasp when she saw it — because she couldn't stand for a single part of her body to still be something he'd once loved.

She'd changed her seat on the train the same week. Three cars back. It hadn't helped, not really — she still saw him twice a day at work, at the desk two rows over from hers, because grief doesn't come with a severance package and rent doesn't care whose heart is broken. She couldn't afford to quit. She couldn't afford, some days, to even breathe normally in his direction. So she didn't look at him, and he mostly didn't look at her, and the two of them orbited the same eleven floors like planets that used to share an atmosphere and now shared nothing but silence.

The train pulled into her station. She got off without looking back at the seat she used to share with someone, the way she did every single day, and told herself, the way she did every single day, that today the not-looking would be easier.

It never was.

## III. What She Couldn't Say

She was, underneath all of it, still a kind person. That was the strange, stubborn truth of her — that heartbreak hadn't hollowed her out entirely. She still stopped for the same limping street dog every morning, still kept dog biscuits in her bag for exactly that purpose. She still stayed forty-five minutes late one Thursday to help a junior colleague fix a spreadsheet formula that wasn't even her problem to fix. People who met her for the first time these days would never have guessed the size of the wound she was walking around with.

But it was there. It sat in her like a low current, humming under everything.

She started therapy in the fourth month. Dr. Nanda's office smelled like sandalwood and had a window that looked out over a parking lot, and Mira spent the first three sessions mostly crying and apologizing for crying, until Dr. Nanda gently told her she could stop apologizing, that this was, in fact, the whole point of the room.

She started the little orange pills not long after — an SSRI whose name she could never remember correctly, that made food taste slightly wrong for the first two weeks and then didn't, that took the jagged edge off her worst mornings without ever quite making the mornings good. She was grateful for it in the dull, functional way you're grateful for a crutch: not happy to need it, just glad it was there.

What she couldn't tell Dr. Nanda — what she couldn't tell anyone, not even Ayesha, especially not Ayesha — was the other thing underneath the anger and the grief. The loneliness that had a body to it. She was twenty-eight and touch-starved in a way that embarrassed her, that felt shameful even to think about while she was supposedly busy being *heartbroken*, as if grief and desire weren't allowed to occupy the same body at the same time. She felt it at odd hours — on the train, in the shower, lying awake at 1 a.m. with the ceiling fan turning lazy shadows across the room — a want with no good place to go.

She tried the apps. Three weeks of them, swiping through men who lied about their height and their marital status and, in one memorable case, their actual name. She met one for coffee who spent the entire forty minutes talking about his ex-wife with a bitterness that made Mira feel, uncomfortably, like she was looking in a mirror. She deleted the apps after the third married man slid into her messages with a hotel address and no shame at all.

She stopped going to parties. Told Ayesha she was tired, which was true, and left out the rest, which was that she couldn't stand one more night of watching couples across a room and feeling the specific, humiliating ache of being the only person alone in it.

Her ex tried, twice, to reach out. A text in the fifth month — *can we talk* — that she stared at for eleven minutes before deleting without answering. A message through Ayesha in the sixth month, gentler, almost pleading — *tell her I just want five minutes, I know I don't deserve it but* — that made something in Mira go cold and flat.

"Don't," she told Ayesha, when Ayesha brought it up, as carefully as she knew how, one evening over instant noodles in Mira's kitchen. "Please. I can't. I feel pathetic enough just thinking about him for two seconds, I don't need five minutes of him doing it in person."

"Okay," Ayesha said, and never brought it up again — though Mira noticed, sometimes, the careful way Ayesha's face moved when his name came up in other contexts, the loyalty she was clearly still trying, quietly, to hold in both hands without dropping either half.

And so there was no one Mira could tell about the loneliness with a body to it. No one to confess to that some nights, after the pills and the tea and the attempts at sleep, she gave in and watched things online she was embarrassed by in the morning — not because the things themselves were shameful, but because of how empty she felt afterward, how the wanting never actually got fed, just briefly, chemically quieted, before the loneliness came back louder than before. She'd lie there afterward feeling more alone than she had before, and she never told a single person, because there was no non-humiliating way to say *I am twenty-eight and touch-starved and grieving and horny and I don't know what to do about any of it.*

## IV. The Circle

The flyer was taped, slightly crooked, to the corkboard of the café near her therapist's office: **A SPACE FOR THE GRIEVING, THE BROKEN, THE IN-BETWEEN.** Thursdays, 7 p.m. A community hall two streets over. No fee, all welcome.

She almost didn't go. She stood outside the hall for four full minutes with her hand on the door, telling herself she could still turn around, that this was the kind of thing other people did, sadder people, more broken people than her — and then she thought about the ceiling fan and the empty apartment waiting for her at the end of the night, and she went in.

The room held about fifteen chairs in a loose circle, half of them full. A woman named Devika ran it — warm-eyed, maybe fifty, the kind of person whose voice made you want to tell her things. She opened with a simple rule: *you can speak or not speak, and either way, no one here will ask you to explain yourself twice.*

People spoke. A man who'd lost his mother eight months ago and still set two plates at dinner without meaning to. A young woman whose fiancé had left three weeks before the wedding and taken the deposit with him. An older man who just cried, quietly, for most of the hour, and no one filled the silence for him, they just let him sit in it, and somehow that felt like the kindest thing Mira had ever watched happen in a room.

And there was him.

He sat at the far end of the circle, still in a way that drew attention precisely by refusing to ask for it — no fidgeting, no glancing at his phone, just a stillness that made everyone else's small movements look loud by comparison. He didn't speak that first night. Mira found herself watching him anyway, the deep, tired shadows under his eyes that looked less like exhaustion and more like something older, something that had simply always been there, and there was — she hated the word even as she thought it — something magnetic in it. A kind of dangerous, sad beauty, the sort you weren't supposed to find in a grief circle, that she felt guilty for noticing at all.

When it was her turn, she told them a compressed version of it — the photo, the breakup, the haircut, the desk two rows over that she couldn't escape. Her voice shook more than she wanted it to. Devika thanked her for sharing, and a woman across the circle reached over and squeezed her hand, and it wasn't much, but it was more human contact than Mira had let herself have in months, and she cried in the car afterward, not from sadness exactly, just from the sheer relief of having been *witnessed*.

She thought about him the whole ride home. Told herself it was nothing. That night, alone, she thought about him again, in ways she was embarrassed by and did anyway, and afterward she caught herself smiling at the ceiling like a teenager, half-disgusted with herself and half not caring at all.

## V. Aarav

She went back the following Thursday for him, and admitted that to herself on the drive over, which felt like its own small, reckless honesty.

He spoke that night. His girlfriend, he told the circle, voice steady and then not steady at all, had died in an accident eighteen months ago — a highway, a truck, a phone call at 11 p.m. that had ended one life and permanently rearranged another. He said her name once, softly, like it cost him something each time he let it out of his mouth, and then he put his face in his hands and cried in a way that made the whole room lean toward him without meaning to. Devika moved to sit beside him. A few others reached out to touch his shoulder. Mira watched and felt an ache in her chest that had nothing to do with pity and everything to do with recognition — *I know that shape of loss, I am wearing a version of it myself* — and something else, too, something she didn't examine too closely.

The following week, a woman named Reema — sharp-eyed, kind, the sort of woman who missed nothing — leaned over during the break and murmured, "You should talk to him. He's been watching you too, you know."

Mira blushed and said no, absolutely not, and then, twenty minutes later, collided with him bodily at the tea table, both of them reaching for the same paper cup, both of them apologizing at the same moment and then laughing at the overlap.

"Aarav," he said, holding out his hand like it was 1950.

"Mira." She shook it and felt, absurdly, like she'd touched something warm after a long time in the cold.

She looked him up on Instagram that night — found him easily enough, a sparse profile, a handful of moody photos of forests and old houses, nothing that gave much away — and hovered over the follow button for a long time before closing the app without pressing it, telling herself she wasn't *that* person.

She didn't need to be. The next Thursday, he asked for her number himself, quietly, after the session, standing a little too close in a way that made her stomach drop pleasantly. She gave it to him before she'd fully decided to.

They started talking. Not the careful, performative talking of the apps, but real talking — long voice notes at midnight, half-finished sentences that picked back up the next morning like no time had passed. She told him things she hadn't told anyone, not even Ayesha, not even Dr. Nanda in the careful, clinical language of a session. She told him about the loneliness with a body to it. He didn't flinch. He listened the way she'd been starving to be listened to — no advice, no fixing, just presence, just letting her cry into the phone at 1 a.m. while he stayed on the line saying almost nothing at all, which turned out to be exactly the right amount to say.

She never asked about his family in any real depth. Never asked exactly what "graphic designer, freelance, works from home for an organization" actually meant on a day-to-day basis, what organization, what hours, what colleagues. It didn't occur to her to ask, or it occurred to her distantly and got waved away, because she was too busy being grateful, too busy feeling, for the first time in a year, *chosen*.

"I've been alone a long time," he told her one night, his voice low over the phone. "After — after her. I stopped believing I'd feel safe with anyone again. And then there was you." A pause, the kind that felt engineered even though she didn't let herself think that at the time. "I want to be with you. Properly. I mean it."

She said yes before he'd technically finished the sentence. She was, if she was honest with herself, desperate to say yes to something.

## VI. The House by the Lake

She told Ayesha only the barest outline — *I'm seeing someone, I'm happy* — and left out his name, left out the grief circle, left out almost everything that mattered. Ayesha, watching her friend smile again in a way she hadn't in over a year, chose not to push, though Mira caught her studying her sometimes with a look that was half happiness and half something more careful.

"Wherever you're going," Ayesha said, the week Mira applied for four days of leave and gave only vague answers about *a trip, out of the city, need to disconnect* — "you can still tell me things. You know that, right? Doesn't matter what happened with — doesn't matter about anything. I'm still your friend. Always."

"I know," Mira said, and meant it, and still didn't tell her anything real, because she didn't want anyone's caution anywhere near the one good thing she had left.

---

The drive out of the city took most of a day. The roads narrowed, then narrowed again, until they were driving beside a river with mountains rising blue-grey in the distance and forest pressing close on both sides. It was beautiful in the way that isolated things are beautiful — and unsettling in exactly the same breath. The last town with an actual shop was, Aarav mentioned casually, about half an hour behind them now. No neighbors after that. No signal, mostly, past a certain bend in the road.

"It's been in my family for generations," he said, when she asked, a little too lightly, whether it wasn't strange to have a house so far from everything. "My grandparents used to use it to get away — camping, disappearing for weeks at a time. It'll just be us now. No people. No noise." He smiled at her sideways. "Isn't that what you said you wanted?"

It was. She'd told him that, more than once, in the middle-of-the-night phone calls — that she was tired of people, tired of crowds, tired of the particular loneliness of being surrounded and unseen. So she let the unease dissolve into something that felt, in the moment, like romance.

He told her more that day than he'd told her in weeks — that he'd lost his parents young, that there'd been no one but his girlfriend for a long time before Mira, that grief had made him strange to himself in ways he was only now starting to unlearn. She kissed his forehead when he said it, the way you'd comfort a child, and asked him to show her the place instead — the garden, gone wild with years of neglect; the lake, visible from the porch as a flat silver coin through the trees.

"Tomorrow," he promised. "I'll take you to the lake tomorrow."

That night he cooked for her — something simple, rice and lentils and a curry that filled the whole cottage with warmth — and disappeared for twenty minutes to come back with a fistful of wildflowers he'd picked in the dark, slightly crushed from his pocket, and it was such a small, silly, romantic gesture that she felt something in her chest loosen that had been clenched for over a year.

She felt, that night, euphoric in a way she hadn't let herself feel since before the photo, before the haircut, before any of it. Like she was living someone else's luckier life. They were happy together, visibly, easily happy, the kind of happy that doesn't need an audience to be real.

## VII. The Woman in White

The lake the next day was cold and clear, ringed by pines, utterly silent except for birdsong and the small sounds they made themselves. They ate lunch on a blanket at the shore. They swam. At some point, mid-laugh, mid-kiss, water still running down both their faces, Mira glanced past his shoulder toward the treeline and went very still.

An old woman stood at the edge of the water. White dress, unmoving, and smiling — smiling in a way that had no warmth in it at all, a smile shaped like an expression rather than a feeling. Something about her face snagged at Mira's memory, familiar in a way she couldn't place, gone before she could look properly, because when she blinked, the woman wasn't there.

"Aarav." Her voice came out too high. "Aarav, there's someone — there was a woman, right there —"

He was out of the water before she finished the sentence, moving toward the treeline with a focus that struck her, even in the moment, as slightly too fast, too complete. He searched for several minutes and found nothing, came back saying it must have been a trick of the light, the shadows of the pines, and she wanted to believe him and mostly did, except for the part of her, small and cold, that didn't.

"Can we go back?" she asked. "I don't feel — I just want to go back to the cottage."

They still had a full day left before they were due to leave. She tried not to think about that as they packed up the blanket and walked back through the woods, her skin prickling the entire way, the sensation of being watched settling over her like something with weight.

Back at the cottage, thirsty, she went into the kitchen for water and found Aarav in the garden through the window, plucking flowers again — for her, she assumed, another gesture like the night before. She smiled, softening, watching him work — until a thorn caught his thumb and drew a bright bead of blood, and instead of wincing, instead of sucking his thumb the ordinary embarrassed way anyone would, he brought it slowly to his mouth and licked it clean with a long, deliberate motion, his eyes half-closed, an expression on his face that had nothing recognizable in it at all.

Every nerve in Mira's body went taut at once. She couldn't have said, in that moment, exactly what she'd seen that was wrong — only that something ancient and animal in her had just woken up screaming, and it did not stop screaming for the rest of the day.

## VIII. Weak Signal

She waited until he was in the shower to try Ayesha. One bar, flickering to zero, flickering back. She typed fast, in fragments, not knowing how much would actually send: *something is wrong here. saw a woman at the lake, gone when I looked twice. aarav did something with his hand, blood, i can't explain it, it scared me. no signal barely. will try to call.* She hit send three times before the bar dropped out completely, and then, distracted by the sound of the shower shutting off, she set the phone down on the kitchen counter and forgot it there entirely.

An hour later and forty kilometers away, Ayesha's phone buzzed with three fragmented messages arriving out of order, and she read them standing in her kitchen with her heart climbing into her throat. She called immediately. It rang and rang and dropped to a flat, mechanical *the number you are trying to reach is switched off.* She called again. Same result. She sat with it for ten unbearable minutes, running through everything she knew — which wasn't much, a general direction, a mention of a lake, a house in the woods — before she remembered, with a lurch of reluctant necessity, that there was exactly one person who might know that stretch of forest well enough to find it fast.

She called Mira's ex.

He picked up on the second ring, and when she explained — haltingly, ashamed even now of needing him for anything — he went quiet for only a second before saying, "I know that area. There's an old house near the lake past Kalpeshwar. My college friends used to dare each other to camp near it — there were stories, bad ones, people who went missing, some cult stuff decades back that never got properly investigated." A pause. "I'm coming. Text me the last thing she sent."

"You don't have to—"

"I'm coming," he said again, and the finality in his voice left no room for argument.

They didn't call the police. Neither of them, in the panic of the moment, thought clearly enough to weigh that option properly — they had a general location, no evidence, and a friend who was, as far as they knew, simply on a romantic weekend away. It felt, in the moment, like an overreaction to loop in strangers. It felt, in the moment, like something they could handle themselves, faster, by driving there directly. It was a decision they would each replay, afterward, for the rest of their lives.

Before they left, Ayesha sent one more message into the dead signal, hoping it would land eventually: *I'm sorry, he's coming too, I know how that sounds, I just need you safe. I love you. Please be okay.*

## IX. The Message That Arrived Too Late

Mira found the phone again over dinner, a soft ding cutting through the quiet of the cottage as one bar of signal briefly resurrected itself somewhere out over the ridge. She read Ayesha's message twice, feeling relief and fury arrive in the same breath — relief that someone was coming, fury that it had to be *him*, that of all people it was her ex driving toward her through the dark.

"What is it?" Aarav asked, watching her face from across the table.

"Nothing." Her voice came out thin. "Work thing."

He studied her a moment longer than she liked, then said he wanted to sleep early, and asked, almost idly, if she was coming to bed.

"Not yet," she said. "I'm not tired. I might just — watch something."

He nodded, unbothered, and went, and the moment the bedroom door closed she was up and pacing, trying the phone again — nothing, the bar gone as fast as it had come — panic climbing into every corner of her chest. What if he found out someone was coming. What if it wrecked things between them before she'd even had the chance to explain that none of this was her doing. What if — and this thought frightened her more than she wanted to admit — what if he reacted badly.

She managed to send one more fragment before the signal died for good: *don't come to the cottage. wait near the lake. please.* She had no way of knowing if it went anywhere at all.

The room swam slightly at the edges. She put it down to exhaustion, to the wine at dinner, to the sheer accumulated weight of the last twelve hours — she didn't want to go to bed, some deep animal part of her insisting she stay awake, stay alert, though she couldn't have said why.

## X. The Bottle

Thirsty again, restless, she went to the kitchen sometime past midnight and, reaching for a glass, saw it — a small brown bottle tucked half behind a row of spice jars, its cap peeking out just enough to catch the light. She pulled it forward.

Sleeping pills. A near-full bottle, the label worn soft from handling, and beside it, half-hidden, a second bottle she didn't recognize at all, its label in a language she couldn't read.

Her stomach dropped through the floor.

She went to the bedroom. Empty. The sheets cool, undisturbed on his side, like he'd never actually lain down at all. She stood in the doorway with her heart hammering, not knowing what to do with what she was looking at, when the lights went out — not flickered, not dimmed, simply cut, all at once, the whole cottage swallowed into a dark so complete it had a texture to it.

She fumbled for her phone, thumbed on the flashlight, the small cone of light doing almost nothing against the size of the dark around her. She searched for candles and found none. She made it as far as the kitchen window, meaning to check whether the lights in the rest of the house — or anywhere at all — were still working, when a scream cut through the night from somewhere out past the trees.

It sounded like Ayesha's voice.

Mira went rigid. Every instinct in her told her to run toward it and every instinct in her told her to run away, and for one paralyzed moment she did neither, just stood there shaking, calling Aarav's name into the empty house, and getting nothing back but her own voice returning to her, thin and small.

## XI. What Was in the Grass

There was no other option in the end but to go outside. Her phone's battery had dropped to eleven percent. No signal, no candle, no husband — no boyfriend, she corrected herself, uselessly, absurdly, in the middle of the worst moment of her life — anywhere to be found. She stepped out onto the porch and into the woods beyond it, calling both their names into the dark, one after the other, her voice cracking further each time.

She'd gone maybe thirty meters when her foot caught on something soft and yielding, and she went down hard onto her knees in the wet grass. She swung the flashlight toward whatever she'd fallen over, already knowing, somehow, in the pit of her stomach, before the light even landed.

It was her ex. Still. Eyes open. Blood soaked black into the ground around him in a spreading stain that the flashlight caught in a dull, wet sheen.

She made a sound she didn't recognize as her own. She said something to him — *you're so stupid, why did you come, why would you come here* — the words tumbling out before her mind had even finished processing what her eyes were telling her, and then, in a single sickening drop, understanding arrived: he hadn't fallen. He hadn't had an accident. Someone had done this to him, deliberately, and that someone was almost certainly still out here in the dark with her.

She ran. Stumbling, sobbing, barely able to see through the flashlight's thin beam and her own blurred vision, she made it back to the cottage and threw herself inside, locking the door behind her out of pure animal instinct, though some distant part of her knew a lock wouldn't matter to whatever this actually was. She didn't stop moving until she was in the bathroom with the door shut and her back against it, both hands pressed over her own mouth to keep the sound in.

## XII. The Door

She heard him come back into the cottage sometime later — footsteps, unhurried, moving from room to room, calling her name in a tone that was, infuriatingly, gently concerned, as if nothing at all had happened.

"Mira? Where'd you go?"

She didn't answer. When he reached the bathroom door and knocked, she asked, in a voice she barely recognized as steady, where he'd been.

"Outside," he said, easy, unbothered. "Smoking. Didn't want to do it in the house. You okay in there?"

"Give me a minute," she managed. "Stomach's upset."

"Take your time." A pause, and then, almost tender: "You feeling alright otherwise?"

She wasn't. The room had begun to tilt at the edges again, her tongue thick and slow, her thoughts arriving half a beat behind where they should have been — the wine, she thought again, except it wasn't the wine, she understood that now with a clarity that scared her more than the confusion did.

He came back a few minutes later, knocked again, asked if she was fine.

"I'm fine," she shouted, too loud, too fast. "Just — give me a minute. Please."

Silence, for a long moment. And then the pounding started — not a knock this time but full-weight blows against the door, the frame shuddering with each one, and she screamed at him to stop, to go away, and heard, on the other side of the door, a laugh — low, delighted, completely unhurried, a laugh with no fear in it at all because there was nothing on this side of the door for him to fear.

"Come on out," he said, still laughing. "It's time."

"Time for what?" Her voice broke apart. "What are you talking about, you're scaring me — I'll call the police, I swear to god—"

"You won't," he said, gently, almost kindly. "There's no signal out here. You know that."

She was crying openly now, asking him, over and over, what he wanted, what any of this meant, and he told her, simply, that he wanted her to come out, and when she screamed that she'd hurt him if he tried to force his way in, he only laughed harder, a sound with no malice in it and somehow, for that reason, worse than malice would have been.

She grabbed for anything — a towel bar, a bottle of something on the sink ledge — her hands too slow, her vision swimming too badly to close around anything useful, and then the door came apart at the hinges in a single splintering crash and he was there, filling the doorway, still smiling, reaching for her, and she swung with everything she had left and connected with nothing, and then his hand caught her across the face and the world went dark.

## XIII. The Circle in the Woods

She surfaced slowly, something cold and wet sprayed across her face, her vision a smear of firelight and shifting shadow, her tongue too thick to shape a scream even as her mind screamed on its own.

For one unbearable, merciful second, she thought she saw Ayesha's face in front of her and felt something in her chest loosen with relief.

Then her vision cleared enough to understand what she was actually looking at, and the relief turned inside out into something with no name. Not Ayesha's face. Ayesha's head, severed, raised on a stake before her, firelight moving across features that had, until very recently, belonged to the person who loved her most in the world.

Mira made a sound that wasn't a scream, wasn't anything human, and then she was vomiting, sobbing, her body trying to reject the sight the same way it would reject poison. Around her, in the firelight, stood four or five figures in plain white cloth — and with a lurch of recognition she understood she'd seen every one of these faces before, in a circle of folding chairs, under fluorescent light, talking about grief.

Aarav crouched in front of her, unhurried, and told her, almost gently, to be quiet, that it would be over soon.

She spat in his face. He wiped it away without any change in his expression and told her, in the same gentle voice, how simply he'd killed the man in the grass, how her friend had begged for her life right up until the moment she stopped being able to, how none of it would have happened at all if Mira hadn't sent those messages, hadn't dragged two more people into these woods behind her — though, he added, almost thoughtfully, shouldn't she be glad, in a way? Wasn't this exactly what some small, unspoken part of her had wanted, all those months of hating him, all those nights wishing him gone?

He laughed then, and it was the first time his laugh had sounded anything less than gentle.

"I loved you," she said, the words scraping out of her raw throat, not sure anymore if they were even true, only sure that she needed him to stop, needed some version of mercy to exist in this clearing.

"I only ever loved my wife," he said, and turned, and called into the dark — and the old woman from the lake stepped into the firelight, the same white dress, the same wrong smile, and Mira understood, with the last clear thought she had left, exactly whose face had been in that photo folder on his phone, the one marked *beloved wife*, the one she'd taken a picture of with shaking hands and never mentioned, not even once.

"She'll be young again," Aarav told the old woman, tender in a way Mira had once mistaken, weeks ago, for love directed at herself. "I found the sacrifice. A sad soul. Exactly what the ritual needs."

The others began to chant, low and rhythmic, and the moon overhead was full and merciless and lit the whole clearing in flat silver light, and the old woman settled in front of Mira with that same creepy, patient smile, waiting.

## XIV. What Was Left of Her

The rope had been tied fast but not carefully — whoever had bound her wrists had been in a hurry, or arrogant enough not to think it mattered. In the pocket of her jeans, forgotten until this exact moment, was the small nail file she carried out of habit, the same one she used every morning without thinking, and her fingers, shaking so badly she nearly dropped it twice, found the edge of the rope and began to saw.

Aarav had stepped back to lead the chant, his eyes closed, his voice rising with the others, and no one was watching her closely enough — grief-drunk, ritual-drunk, certain of their victory — when the rope finally gave way.

She moved before she'd consciously decided to. The old woman's eyes opened first, some ancient reflex catching the movement, and she lunged — faster than her frail body should have allowed — and Mira, screaming, terrified, driven by something past thought, drove the nail file into the side of the old woman's throat with both hands and all the strength grief and fear had left in her.

Blood came fast and dark in the firelight. The old woman folded to the ground without a sound.

The chanting broke apart into chaos — people falling to their knees, wailing, some of them scrambling toward the body, none of them, in that first shattered moment, moving toward Mira at all. Aarav's scream tore through the clearing, raw and enormous, nothing gentle left in it now, and he threw himself down beside the old woman's body, shaking her, calling a name that wasn't his beloved's, that Mira would never learn, begging her to wake up.

Then he stood, and turned toward Mira with an expression that had nothing human left in it, and took one step — and caught his foot in the same length of rope that had held her, and went down hard, and a burning stick from the edge of the firepit caught him through the throat as he fell.

He didn't get back up.

## XV. After

Mira didn't remember most of the walk out. She remembered the cold. She remembered the sky going from black to grey to a bruised, indifferent blue as the night finally, finally ended. She remembered the mourners behind her, still kneeling in the ashes of their fire, still weeping, none of them chasing her, as if whatever had held them together had died with the two bodies in the clearing.

She reached the main road sometime after dawn and stood at its edge until a truck slowed and stopped, the driver taking one look at her — blood-streaked, hollow-eyed, barefoot — and not asking a single question before driving her straight to the nearest station.

She told the police everything. All of it, in an order that made no sense the first time and had to be told again, more slowly, with a detective who kept looking at her like he didn't quite believe the shape of what he was hearing even as the evidence, over the following days, confirmed every word.

---

Months later, at the office, there was a small memorial in the break room for the man who used to sit two rows over from her desk — a framed photo, a few flowers, a card people signed without knowing what to write. Mira stood in front of it longer than she meant to.

Ayesha survived. Found half-conscious near the lake's edge, exactly where her final message had begged Mira to tell her to wait, spared by nothing but the fact that she'd never quite made it as far as the cottage. She carried scars, some of them visible, most of them not, and she and Mira never spoke about that night without first, wordlessly, reaching for each other's hands.

Neither of them went back to grief circles. Neither of them, for a long time, could stand to be in a room of folding chairs at all.

---

Somewhere across the city, months after that, in a room with folding chairs and a flyer taped crookedly to a corkboard, a new circle was gathering. A new woman sat with her grief folded in her lap like something she didn't yet know how to put down, and across the room, a young man sat very still, with eyes that looked like they hadn't slept in years.

An old woman leaned toward the new girl, smiling in a way that had no warmth in it at all, and nodded toward him.

"You should talk to him," she said softly. "He understands loss better than anyone here."
